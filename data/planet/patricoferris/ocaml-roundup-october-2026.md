<html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">Lots going in October, including:</html:p><html:ul xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml"><html:li><html:p>Attending <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/soocon26-belfast">SOOCon26 in Belfast</html:a></html:span></html:p></html:li><html:li><html:p><html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/ppxlib-merge-derivers">Merging derivers in ppxlib</html:a></html:span></html:p></html:li><html:li><html:p>Updating <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/graft">Graft</html:a></html:span> to <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/forester">Forester</html:a></html:span> 6.0</html:p></html:li><html:li><html:p>Building a <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/claudius-playground">playground</html:a></html:span> for <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/mdales">Michael</html:a></html:span>'s <html:code>Claudius</html:code> library.</html:p></html:li><html:li><html:p>Reviewing <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/outreachy">Outreachy</html:a></html:span> contributions.</html:p></html:li></html:ul><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h2>Weeklies</h2></header><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h3>Shells, SOOCon26 and Part IIs</h3></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">Last week I gave a precise meaning to the "mergeability" of container images. The further I go down this rabbit hole, the less convinced I am of the exact benefits of such an approach. However, there are lots of off-shoots to explore as well, which all seem very worthwhile and might have better semantics than merging (e.g. incremental computation and provenance-tracking).</html:p><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h4>Uniform Representations</h4></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">Whilst working on things related to <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/merry">Merry</html:a></html:span> and <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/shelter">Shelter</html:a></html:span>, I couldn't help but think how much easier parts of this problem would be if we were using <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_9_from_Bell_Labs">Plan9</html:a></html:span> for one simple reason: the uniform access to OS resources. Half the battle on Linux is trying to trace or sandbox a process with respect to some set of OS resources (in an ideal world, all of them). Syscalls are perhaps the closest thing we have, but there isn't a one-to-one mapping between user actions and syscalls (e.g. opening a file using <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://kernel.dk/io_uring.pdf">io_uring</html:a></html:span> does not call <html:code>open(2)</html:code> or <html:code>openat(2)</html:code>).</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">I think there is often a trade-off here in terms of performance and simplicity. Plan9's 9P protocol was not exactly known for its performance. But, I still find myself, to use a phrase my colleague <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/ryangibb">Ryan</html:a></html:span> once used when explaining his use of <html:code>emacs</html:code>, <html:em>comfy</html:em> with uniform representations. Another example, <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://www.forester-notes.org/tfmt-000R/index.xml">a tree in Forester</html:a></html:span>.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">It is pleasant when these representations have a defined structure, even if the contents are more malleable. For instance, I don't want everything to be schema-less JSON. <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/forester">Forester</html:a></html:span> trees have <html:em>tags</html:em> and that allows me to impose a schema in a structured way that doesn't lead to parsing errors. Similarly, <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://9fans.github.io/plan9port/man/man9/intro.html">9P offers a simple protocol</html:a></html:span> on which all things are built. As I understand it, the semantics are given by the interpretation of the file server that is resolved, but the protocol is uniform. Anyway, just some thoughts.</html:p></section><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h4>State of Open Conference 2026 -- Belfast</h4></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">This week I was fortunate enough to attend (as a volunteer) SOOCon26 in Belfast in the Ulster Bank building, just opposite the City Hall. Thanks to <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/anilmadhavapeddy">Anil Madhavapeddy</html:a></html:span> for linking me up with <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://amandabrock.com/">Amanda Brock</html:a></html:span> to make it happen at the last hour.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">If I can boil down the conference into a general topic it would be: how are large language models and coding agents impacting commercial, business-oriented open-source software. I found the conference's near-total focus on AI a little surprising and a little disappointing, but I suppose it reflects the current state of open source. For example, most, if not all, the working developers and business owners at this conference, for one reason or another, felt the need to relate their work to AI, both in their talks and in hallway conversations. Something I continue to struggle with, though, is that almost all references to "AI" pointed to large, expensive, <html:em>closed</html:em> frontier models (such as Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot and Gemini). That being said, there were talks on data (and AI) sovereignty as well as mentions of the increasingly capabale open models (e.g. Qwen).</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">I would like to add that the conference was essentially offline: no recordings or streaming. With that in mind, I want to respect the fact that most attendees and presenters could speak freely and candidly, so I will only focus on some of the higher level threads of discussion without attributing too much to any individual.</html:p><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h5>AI: Productivity</h5></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">The morning focused mainly on the question: <html:em>what is a software developer's job in 2026?</html:em> This culminated in a panel discussion on "building trust in AI". Already, I would say the conference's mind was made up, AI is here to stay; it is just a tool; we should learn how to hold it properly. I think, in part, this was mainly due to the group of speakers who were all, more or less, executives within for-profit businesses that incorporate open source in some way: <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://github.com/">Github</html:a></html:span>, <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://bimp.ai/">BIMP.ai</html:a></html:span> and <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://budibase.com/">Budibase</html:a></html:span>. The only exception was a speaker from the <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://www.iod.com/">Institute of Directors</html:a></html:span>. I think this gave the morning session a commercial flavour which suggested the tech industry's large-scale adoption of AI tooling, a position I'm not sure is as common among, or available to, smaller open-source maintainers.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">One stream of thought that was popular was arguing that <html:em>languages like C, where it is easy to introduce memory bugs and vulnerabilities, are on the way out</html:em>. <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/migrating-the-github-copilot-runtime-to-rust-using-copilot/">Copilot's runtime rewrite in Rust</html:a></html:span> was championed as a success story for the kinds of productivity that can be unlocked for highly-skilled developers. A pinch of salt is needed though, as Microsoft did sponsor the event. My own thoughts, however, are that there are quite a few success stories we can learn from where software was translated from one programming language to another using an LLM. Migration seems well-defined and well-scoped, often with large, pre-existing test suites that can easily guide a tool towards some form of correctness. In the same breath, it is not necessarily that straightforward, for example, see <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://lobste.rs/s/caubyr/bun_1_4_rust_rewrite_is_not_looking_good">some lobste.rs commentary on the Bun rewrite in Rust</html:a></html:span>.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">Another undercurrent throughout the morning talks was how LLMs could <html:em>free up developers to actively engage with users</html:em>. Some quotes from the talks included "experiment with empathy" and "AI doesn't write the story". On the surface, this seems like a reasonable argument. But, shouldn't these large teams of people have already been thinking about their users, with or without AI? I'm also not convinced this free time will appear. When the metric of success is at least proportional to the rate of new features, it would seem that a tool, like an LLM, would only crank out more widgets rather than better widgets.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">A final worrying conversation from the panel, discussed the fact that companies are <html:em>not hiring graduates</html:em>. This was self-evident from a few other people I met in and around the conference: computer science graduates still looking for jobs.</html:p></section><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h5>AI: Community, Data Centres and Vulnerabilities</h5></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">The post-lunch session included a talk about the future of community open source, a talk about AI data centres and finally a panel about the "vulnpocalypse".</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">The first talk focused on the realities of doing community open source and how, in effect, AI and LLMs are not (yet) a part of our shared open culture, citing large, anti-AI open-source projects, some which have banned AI contributions (e.g. <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://nlnetlabs.nl/llm-policy/">NLNet Labs</html:a></html:span>, <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://code.haverbeke.berlin/codemirror/dev/src/branch/main/CONTRIBUTING.md#contributing-code">Codemirror</html:a></html:span>) and pro-AI projects which have closed contributions (e.g. <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://ladybird.org/posts/changing-how-we-develop-ladybird/">Ladybird</html:a></html:span>). Of course, open source never meant <html:em>the right to contribute</html:em>, but it still signals a, perhaps, worrying shift in open-source, cultural norms. <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07542">An arXiv paper collected some AI policies on popular repositories which is worth a skim</html:a></html:span>.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">Another topic in the talk was the "time to open". The speaker argued that it was now possible to completely reverse engineer closed-source products and tools in as little as 48 hours. This could lead to <html:em>more</html:em> open source business models, as closed models are no longer viable. The speaker suggested that the open-source community around a project is now more important than the code itself. I think I fall somewhere closer to believing that they are both important. I worry about the proliferation of agentically-managed software stacks which companies and individuals use with little incentive to upstream patches back to the community (or release their new code for the community). This kind of isolationism seems more likely given the ability to create your own implementation of any piece of software.</html:p><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h6>Data Centres</h6></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">The talk on hyperscalar data centres presented some <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://aprs.scot/data-centre-map/">great work from the folks at <html:em>Action to Protect Rural Scotland</html:em> (APRS)</html:a></html:span>. With some serious energy projections:</html:p><html:blockquote xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml"><html:p>This gives an estimated around 7,306 MW currently in the planning system or in pre-application consultation. Peak winter electricity demand in Scotland is just over 4,000 MW.</html:p></html:blockquote><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">It seemed like the feeling in the room was that these data centres are inevitable, in spite of these jarring numbers; and that somewhere down the line, the ends will be just so good, that the means will be justified.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">It seems, once again, I must reach for <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://youtu.be/oHSnUqzyiGM?list=RDoHSnUqzyiGM">Pete Seeger</html:a></html:span>.</html:p><html:blockquote xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml"><html:p>Perhaps the biggest change will come when we don't have to change much at all When maniacs holler "grow, grow, grow, grow!"; we can choose to be small.</html:p></html:blockquote></section><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h6>Vulnerabilities</h6></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">I don't think this needs too much explaining, but visit <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://mastodon.social/@bagder">Daniel Stenberg's mastodon feed</html:a></html:span> and that will catch you up. Edit: I wanted to also add a more <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/10/12/the-2026-curl-team/">recent post from Daniel</html:a></html:span> highlighting the other work of the core <html:code>curl</html:code> team.</html:p></section></section><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h5>Conclusion of sorts</h5></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">Unfortunately, I missed a good portion of the final session as I was helping clear up. The talks were: <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://stateofopencon2026.sched.com/event/2YedV/keynote-michael-shanks-founder-and-ceo-budibase-the-cost-of-sovereignty">"The cost of Sovereignty"</html:a></html:span>, <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://stateofopencon2026.sched.com/event/2Yecs/fireside-chat-knowledge-graphs-for-digital-transformation">"Knowledge Graphs for Digital Transformation"</html:a></html:span> and <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://stateofopencon2026.sched.com/event/2YefG/keynote-rebecca-lutton-head-of-technology-adoption-digital-catapult-you-cant-download-trust">"You can't download trust"</html:a></html:span>.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">I managed to have a good chat with <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://openuk.uk/profiles/phil-weir/">Phil Weir</html:a></html:span>, director of <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://flaxandteal.co.uk/">Flax and Teal</html:a></html:span>, about some of their work including <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://github.com/flaxandteal/alizarin">Alizarin</html:a></html:span>, a tool for working with knowledge graphs and <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://www.archesproject.org/">Arches</html:a></html:span> data. It was refreshing to hear about the details of an actual project someone was building.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">Ultimately, I would say the day left me disheartened for the immediate future of (open-source) software. What I found most unsettling about SOOCon is what I find unsettling about the tech industry at large. It would seem that the industry is leaving new graduates in the lurch. Additionally, environmental concerns are falling somewhere (far?) below commerical concerns.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">I'm also anxious that open source's ethos is being eclipsed by the need for companies (and individual employees) to demonstrate their relevance in an AI-focused economy, and that that performance of relevance is <html:em>really</html:em> about integrating, into whatever you're doing, the products coming out of a few powerful companies, which are not invested in the values which make open source make sense.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">I think conferences like <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://stateofopencon.com/">State of Open</html:a></html:span> are important, and we need more conversations from all kinds of open-source community members. I'd like to see more talks from students, sole maintainers of highly depended-on software, folks from other non-profit-driven open-source initiatives like <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://www.outreachy.org/">Outreachy</html:a></html:span> or the <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://processingfoundation.org/">Processing Foundation</html:a></html:span> (these are just examples I know). I recognise that, perhaps, SOOCon26 Belfast was suffering from being in Belfast, hardly the most connected metropolis in the world... but it is the home of <html:em>The Big Fish</html:em>. I'm excited to hopefully see it return in the years to come.</html:p><html:div xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml" style="text-align: center;">

Here, "<html:em>safest</html:em>", can have lots of meanings: the best lit road, a route with tactile paving or accessible pedestrian signals etc.</html:p><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">In addition to this, we may wish to provide suggestions for how to improve the current <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/gruber2007ontology"><html:em>folksonomy</html:em></html:a></html:span> of information OSM stores.</html:p></section><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h5>From OCaml to Graphics Shader Languages</h5></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">This project wants to reuse OCaml's typechecker to provide a safe way to create graphics shaders. As an extension, a ppx rewriter will provide a translation layer from OCaml to the embedded DSL making it possible to write shaders in a subset of the OCaml language.</html:p><html:blockquote xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml"><html:p>When interacting with the GPU for graphics programming, parts of the rendering pipeline are written as standalone shader programs. The standard method of using these is to store the shader source code as a string, which is then compiled at runtime by the GPU driver. This is not a performance issue, as shader programs are usually small enough that compilation time is negligible. However, it does mean simple syntax errors or incorrect types, which would normally be caught at compilation, won’t manifest until the entire program is run.</html:p><html:p>I propose a new library, Shaders_of_ocaml, allowing for shader programs to be written inline as native OCaml, which is then compiled to shader code strings to be passed to the existing graphics libraries. This would allow the OCaml compiler to provide type checking for every shader written.</html:p><html:p>-- From Rohan's Proposal</html:p></html:blockquote></section><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h5>Ripple: An OCaml Library for Incremental Computation</h5></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">Following the work of <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/acar2002adaptive">Adaptive Functional Programming</html:a></html:span> and <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/chen2011implicit">Implicit self-adjusting computation</html:a></html:span>, this project will build an incremental computation engine in OCaml alongside an expressive combinator library for build such computations. As an extension, a small DSL will be built that makes use of the engine to build an implicitly incremental programming language.</html:p><html:blockquote xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml"><html:p>The core of this project is to implement an eDSL and a runtime in OCaml for self-adjusting computation. I also propose a new TDT which allows for generic monoidal folds over modifiable sequences: the library will include a native foldMap primitive that allows performant reduction over a modifiable sequence of modifiable values for any general monoid, giving logarithmic updates with a small trace. I will then compare foldMap to the generic fold implemented in plain SAC.</html:p><html:p>-- From Boris' Proposal</html:p></html:blockquote></section></section><section class="block hide-metadata"><header><h4>Ppxlib </h4></header><html:p xmlns:html="http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml">Just a quick footnote about <html:span class="link local"><html:a href="/ppxlib">Ppxlib</html:a></html:span>: I opened a PR to <html:span class="link external"><html:a href="https://github.com/ocaml-ppx/ppxlib/pull/654">merge multiple instances of a deriver</html:a></html:span>.
