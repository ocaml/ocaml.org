<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#the-uk-label-catalogue-on-foundry" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>The UK label catalogue on Foundry</h3>
<p>The <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/2026w44">UK label database</a> I started a few weeks ago has grown quite a bit, and now lives on an internal (password-protected, for licensing reasons) server that I've been showing to <a href="https://mynameismwd.org">Michael Dales</a> and <a href="https://aneeshnaik.github.io/">Aneesh Naik</a> to get their feedback. It's currently at 600 major data layers (!) for the UK from 74 sources, with around 75 million vector features, all mapped into PMTiles and GeoParquet with their provenance and licences attached. The broad classes are:</p>
<ul>
<li>Forests and trees: the <a href="https://data-forestry.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/25690ca444d54a588394d61a985006b5_0">National Forest Inventory 2023</a> (641k polygons across 25 classes from broadleaved to windblow), 36 million <a href="https://environment.data.gov.uk/dataset/9c41b3c6-2453-44f6-9900-e7821f1a1072">trees outside woodland</a>, <a href="https://naturalengland-defra.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/Defra::ancient-woodland-england">ancient woodland</a>, the <a href="https://ati.woodlandtrust.org.uk/tree-search/">Ancient Tree Inventory</a> and <a href="https://www.treeregister.org/champion-trees-map/">champion trees</a>, along with species labels from forest stands and <a href="https://data-forestry.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/felling-licence-applications-england">felling licences</a>.</li>
<li>Habitats, soils and crops: the <a href="https://naturalengland-defra.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/Defra::living-england-habitat-map-phase-4">Living England habitat map</a> (6.9 million polygons), <a href="https://naturalengland-defra.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/Defra::priority-habitats-inventory-england">Priority Habitats</a>, <a href="https://naturalengland-defra.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/Defra::england-peat-map-peaty-soil-extent-1">peaty soils</a>, <a href="https://naturalengland-defra.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/Defra::agricultural-land-classification-alc-grades-post-1988-england">agricultural land classification</a>, <a href="https://www.isric.org/explore/soilgrids">SoilGrids</a> at 250 m, and the <a href="https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/crop-map-of-england-crome-2024">Crop Map of England</a> for every year from 2016 to 2024.</li>
<li>Water: about 180 layers covering Environment Agency <a href="https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/rivers-and-sea-defended-and-undefended-flood-risk-extents-present-day">flood risk</a> extents and depths, four cycles of <a href="https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/wfd-river-water-body-catchments-cycle-4-classification-2025-full-resolution">Water Framework Directive</a> water bodies, <a href="https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/statutory-main-river-map">main</a> and <a href="https://ckan.publishing.service.gov.uk/dataset/chalk-rivers-england">chalk</a> rivers, reservoirs and their stocks, <a href="https://environment.data.gov.uk/hydrology/explore">groundwater</a>, <a href="https://environment.data.gov.uk/bwq/profiles/">bathing waters</a>, <a href="https://www.streamwaterdata.co.uk/">storm overflow spills</a>, and <a href="https://www.hydrosheds.org/hydroatlas">HydroATLAS</a> and <a href="https://catalogue.ceh.ac.uk/documents/9a46d428-958f-4ac1-86eb-94eee70c0955">CAMELS-GB</a> catchments.</li>
<li>Freshwater ecology: <a href="https://environment.data.gov.uk/ecology/explorer/downloads/">fish surveys</a> back to 1973, plus macroinvertebrate, macrophyte and diatom survey sites.</li>
<li>Designations: <a href="https://naturalengland-defra.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/Defra::sites-of-special-scientific-interest-england">SSSIs</a>, <a href="https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/67b4ef48-d0b2-4b6f-b659-4efa33469889/ramsar-england">Ramsar sites</a>, <a href="https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/726484b0-d14e-44a3-9621-29e79fc47bfc/national-nature-reserves-england">National Nature Reserves</a>, RSPB <a href="https://opendata-rspb.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/RSPB::rspb-reserves">reserves</a> and <a href="https://opendata-rspb.opendata.arcgis.com/datasets/RSPB::ibas-uk">Important Bird Areas</a>, and <a href="https://open-data-national-trust.hub.arcgis.com/datasets/National-Trust::national-trust-open-data-land-always-open">National Trust</a> and <a href="https://scottishwildlifetrust.org.uk/our-work/our-evidence-base/our-data/">Scottish Wildlife Trust</a> land.</li>
<li>Climate: 280 <a href="https://catalogue.ceda.ac.uk/uuid/789b3065d74a4c948ab05d33556c86d0">HadUK-Grid</a> 1 km layers of rainfall, temperature, frost and wet days back to 1970, and every weather attribution study I could find from <a href="https://www.worldweatherattribution.org/analyses/">World Weather Attribution</a>, <a href="https://interactive.carbonbrief.org/attribution-studies/">Carbon Brief</a> and <a href="https://www.climameter.org/hazard-database">ClimaMeter</a>.</li>
<li>People: the 2025 <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/english-indices-of-deprivation-2025">Indices of Deprivation</a>, <a href="https://www.nomisweb.co.uk/sources/census_2021_bulk">Census 2021</a> health and qualifications, <a href="https://get-information-schools.service.gov.uk/">schools</a> with their Ofsted outcomes, and GP practices with their recorded diabetes, depression and hypertension <a href="https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/publications/statistical/quality-and-outcomes-framework-achievement-prevalence-and-exceptions-data">prevalence</a>.</li>
<li>And the rest: EA <a href="https://environment.data.gov.uk/dataset/13787b9a-26a4-4775-8523-806d13af58fc">LiDAR</a> terrain, surface and vegetation heights, <a href="https://environment.data.gov.uk/dataset/7a955570-d465-11e4-a37c-f0def148f590">landfill sites</a>, <a href="https://uk-air.defra.gov.uk/data/">air quality monitoring stations</a>, and the National Trust's <a href="https://open-data-national-trust.hub.arcgis.com/">coastal land use surveys</a> from 1965 and 2014.</li>
</ul>
<p>What's been really fun is building autoresearch loops for Tessera downstream tasks against these labels. The site now has explorers for UK land cover, tree species and canopy change, early <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/ideas/tessera-ghost-roads">ghost roads</a>, daily road traffic, and even <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/ideas/sdms-with-cnns">SDMs</a> for the chances of catching each of 22 freshwater fish species in every river reach. I'm really excited about the prospect of systematically evaluating these against the sorts of <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/ideas/tessera-habitat-maps">habitat maps</a> that <a href="https://mynameismwd.org">Michael Dales</a> and <a href="https://aneeshnaik.github.io/">Aneesh Naik</a> have <a href="https://www.aneeshnaik.com/blogposts/20261124_weeknotes_2026_47.html">been producing</a>. The water labels are also proving useful for the hydrology work that <a href="https://github.com/ctk21">Tom Kelly</a> is doing, as he's been writing up <a href="https://ctk21.github.io/2026/11/16/data-for-rainfall-runoff">what data rainfall–runoff models need</a>.</p>

<h2><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#was-software-always-too-complex-for-humans" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>Was software always too complex for humans?</h2>
<p>After yet another week of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis#Alp%C3%B6ge_alleged_proof">AI drama</a> and security issues, I pushed out a polemic on whether "<a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/software-too-complex-for-humans">software was just too complex for humans to tackle</a>". I've been musing on whether we will (probably sooner than we think!) end up deriving all executable software from formal specifications.</p>
<p>It got some great responses, including Ian Betteridge <a href="https://x.com/IanMBetteridge/status/2124899729074999394">giving permission</a> to break <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betteridge's_law_of_headlines">his law of headlines</a>. First <a href="https://mynameismwd.org">Michael Dales</a> <a href="https://digitalflapjack.com/weeknotes/2026-11-23/">argued back</a> that LLMs are just an accelerant of an existing systemic problem:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>We build software beyond the limits of our capabilities to understand/manage, and we have done so willingly in the seek of other forms of success beyond correctness.
<cite>-- <a href="https://digitalflapjack.com/weeknotes/2026-11-23/">Michael Dales</a>, November 2026</cite></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Michael's obviously quite right that not all software is written to be "correct" (for some strict specification). The trouble is that we can't easily indicate its intended lifetime. Isn't it weird that one of my weekend shell scripts, and firmware for an embedded pacemaker, are basically managed and (often) licensed the same way?
We're going to need ways to mark what code is <em>for</em> and how much it <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/opam-ai-disclosure-update">should be trusted</a>.</p>
<p>I'm still chewing through Michael's point that before going full formal-spec-heavy we first need to do a better job of <em>acceptance</em>.  <a href="https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~comar/">Cyrus Omar</a>'s work on <a href="https://hazel.org">Hazel</a> allows for iterative, interactive refinement of specifications which seems similar to an acceptance testing process. (Incidentally, <a href="https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~comar/">Cyrus Omar</a> will be delivering next week's <a href="https://www.cst.cam.ac.uk/seminars/wednesday">departmental seminar</a> on a similar topic)</p>
<p>There was lively <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601823">online discussion</a> too. Several commenters called the idea of deriving everything from specifications naive:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Formal specifications can work in controlled environments like math or small software projects with a tight group of contributors to subscribe to specific goals and constraints. But the vast majority of software is not like that, it exists because it does something that people find useful for some real world purpose, and those real world purposes are messy and unspecifiable.
<cite>-- <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50624843">dasil003 on Hacker News</a>, November 2026</cite></p>
</blockquote>
<p>This is true of much business logic, but other domains are more tractable. With networked protocols, the key trick is that a formal spec doesn't have to pin down <em>one</em> correct behaviour, but can instead describe the <em>range</em> of allowable actions. For example, in our <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/papers/2015-sosp-sibylfs">formal spec of POSIX filesystems from 2015</a>, we wrote that renaming a file onto a non-empty directory can validly return any of <code>EISDIR</code>, <code>EEXIST</code> or <code>ENOTEMPTY</code> depending on the order an implementation makes its checks, or of course <code>readdir</code> returns entries in any order. Rather than picking one behaviour, the formal spec validates the 'shape' of the responses:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>A sound specification must be loose enough to accommodate all such variation (looseness should not be confused with the question of whether a specification is precise: we want a mathematically precise model, but one that admits a range of allowable behaviours).
<cite>-- <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/papers/2015-sosp-sibylfs">SibylFS: formal specification and oracle-based testing for POSIX and real-world file systems</a>, SOSP 2015</cite></p>
</blockquote>
<p>This approach let us check lots of real traces across Linux, macOS and FreeBSD against one formal model. Network protocols are admittedly a sweet spot here, but you gotta start somewhere...</p>
<p>Not everyone was convinced by the <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/papers/2025-internet-ecology">ecological diversity</a> argument either:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>What evidence is there that a diverse system is better than a homogenous one? This is pretty non-sensical.
<cite>-- <a href="https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50604659">prestonvasquez on Hacker News</a>, November 2026</cite></p>
</blockquote>
<p>There's some (if not a lot of) evidence, and also a commonsense intuition. A recent <a href="https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790769">CHI 2026 paper</a> is quite a dense read full of jargon, but it points out the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_worm">Morris worm</a> as an early example. That worm never took down the <em>entire</em> Internet in 1988, since it could only infect VAX and Sun-3 machines (hah!) running Berkeley Unix.</p>
<p>Bruce Schneier <a href="https://www.schneier.com/essays/archives/2010/11/the_dangers_of_a_sof.html">revisited this in 2010</a> and noted that while a monoculture is dangerous, diversity is also costly:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Sure, it would be great if a corporate IT department ran half Windows and half Linux, or half Apache and half Microsoft IIS, but doing so would require more expertise and cost more money. [...] A single operating system locked down by experts is far more secure than two operating systems configured by sysadmins who aren't so expert.
<cite>-- <a href="https://www.schneier.com/essays/archives/2010/11/the_dangers_of_a_sof.html">Bruce Schneier, The Dangers of a Software Monoculture</a>, 2010</cite></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Bruce's other objection was that a couple of operating systems and two or three router vendors only buy a very limited amount of diversity, compared to the millions of genomes in a biological population. In the intervening years, coding agents change all that! The cost of maintaining many implementations and the expertise needed to lock each one down is dropping like a rock. I'd be amazed if we don't have multiple usable full operating systems inside of a year, possibly <a href="https://doi.org/10.1145/3552326.3587458">plucking code from Linux</a>.</p>
<p>I'm "blogging out loud" here to get my thoughts together, so thanks everyone for taking the trouble to give me feedback! I'm struggling to keep up with the socials right now, so I've also added a <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/software-too-complex-for-humans#page-socials">social comment tracker</a> to this site so it's easy to see it all in one place.</p>
<h2><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#but-crafting-software-isnt-entirely-dead-yet" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>But crafting software isn't entirely dead yet...</h2>
<p>Having sounded the software doomtrain earlier, there's still a lot of work left in artisanal programming interfaces. I've been working on getting <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/eio">Eio</a>'s Windows backend to align with the (quite POSIX-biased) portable layer of Eio. This is primarily because <a href="https://jonmsterling.com">Jon Sterling</a> wanted <a href="https://www.forester-notes.org">Forester</a> to work well on Windows, and I'm a <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/forester-teaching-notes">Forester user</a> and so the completionist in me wants to get it done!</p>
<p>Also, <a href="https://patrick.sirref.org">Patrick Ferris</a> put tons of time into the Eio Windows backend and I want to make sure his efforts are usable in the wild by finishing "the last 20%".  Windows, however, is just full of legacy interfaces that are often decades old, so my understanding remains imperfect at best...</p>
<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#switching-to-windows-iocp" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>Switching to Windows IOCP</h3>
<p>I've finally opened a PR for my long-running <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/eio/pull/996">attempt to switch Eio_windows to use IO completion ports</a>. This builds on <a href="https://patrick.sirref.org">Patrick Ferris</a>'s original <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/ocaml-iocp">IOCP bindings</a>, which I've <a href="https://github.com/avsm/ocaml-iocp">extended</a> for feature completeness and to deal with the many Windows quirks.</p>
<p>The structure I settled on is similar to the Linux one, with each Eio domain getting its own completion port for file, pipe, TCP and UDP operations. Completions are delivered directly back to the submitting scheduler, so it's fairly performant in multi-domain settings as well. Cancellations, as always, are quite fiddly since both the fds and associated buffer must stay live until the kernel completes the operation. Median throughput on an 8 vCPU Windows VM (kindly set up by <a href="https://www.tunbury.org/">Mark Elvers</a>) is consistently faster than Eio main, and I expect a future <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/projects/oxcaml">OxCaml</a> port will be even faster:</p>

<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#windows-ioring-goes-nowhere" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>Windows IoRing goes nowhere</h3>
<p>Since <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/ocaml-uring">io_uring</a> has been <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/icfp25-post-posix">such a win on Linux</a>, I also built experimental <a href="https://tangled.org/anil.recoil.org/ocaml-ioring">ocaml-ioring</a> bindings to the <a href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/ioringapi/">Windows I/O ring API</a>.</p>
<p>In theory, this is extremely similar to the Linux shared rings and follows the same queue and completion model. The similarity extends to supporting registered file and buffer tables. Unfortunately, despite my best efforts I just haven't been able to get any performance benefit out of them in real applications <em>vs</em> using IOCP, even for sustained batch transfers! The IORing APIs also only cover file operations, so IOCP is still needed for sockets regardless.</p>
<p>So I'm regretfully just going to put these bindings on ice, but if you do manage to make IORing go vroom on Windows, please let me know! Probably just some scheduler tweaking or something needed.</p>
<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#hardening-the-linux-io-uring-interfaces" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>Hardening the Linux io-uring interfaces</h3>
<p><a href="https://roscidus.com">Thomas Leonard</a> also released <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/eio/releases/tag/v1.7">Eio 1.7</a>, which includes fixes for several obscure but important sandbox escapes. Over in <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/ocaml-uring">ocaml-uring</a>, we've been applying a series of hardening fixes to the bindings, such as rejecting <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/ocaml-uring/pull/172">NUL bytes in paths</a> and <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/ocaml-uring/pull/173">excess buffers</a>. I also fixed <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/ocaml-uring/pull/179">signal handling in Uring.wait</a> and <a href="https://github.com/ocaml-multicore/ocaml-uring/pull/180">zero-initialised the statx buffer</a>. These are mostly edge cases, but important for robustness in a busy system.</p>
<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#using-this-in-my-home-router" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>Using this in my home router</h3>
<p>The popular home router maker GL.iNet <a href="https://www.gl-inet.com/en-gb/blogs/blog/glinet-security-notice-2026-11-20">disclosed</a> that attackers had exploited a vulnerability in its GoodCloud remote management service to install malware on some connected routers and KVMs.
Luckily, on a hot tip from <a href="https://toao.com">Sadiq Jaffer</a>, <a href="https://dave.recoil.org">Dave Scott</a>, <a href="https://nick.recoil.org">Nick Ludlam</a> and I had already been switching to <a href="https://openwrt.org/toh/gl.inet/gl-mt6000">Flint 2</a> routers, but running upstream <a href="https://openwrt.org">OpenWrt</a> rather than trusting some random vendor firmware!</p>
<p>The brand new <a href="https://tangled.org/recoil.org">recoil.org</a> <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/tangled-and-ci">Tangled</a> organisation has two very experimental repositories so far. Our <a href="https://tangled.org/recoil.org/openwrt">openwrt</a> tree tracks the latest upstream 7.4-rc1 kernel with the wifi drivers ported across, and <a href="https://tangled.org/recoil.org/recoilwrt">recoilwrt</a> flashes it onto a router along with a simple OxCaml service installer.</p>
<p>I'm using the latter to drop in a DNS and WireGuard layer from my <a href="https://tangled.org/anil.recoil.org/ox">monorepo</a>, building on the <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/oxcaml-packages">OxCaml packaging</a> and (very satisfyingly) using <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/icfp25-post-posix">Eio+io-uring</a> in my home network. Our continued efforts to <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/recoil-self-hosting-2026">self-host the Recoil collective</a> from the ground up are gathering sufficient pace that what we started in 1997 might actually finish by 2027...</p>

<h2><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#literature-scanning" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>Literature scanning</h2>
<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#andrea-manica-becomes-a-turing-ai-pioneer" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>Andrea Manica becomes a Turing AI Pioneer</h3>
<p>Big congratulations to my colleague <a href="https://www.zoo.cam.ac.uk/people/andrea-manica">Andrea Manica</a> in Zoology, who is <a href="https://www.ukri.org/news/ukri-invests-in-20-turing-ai-fellows-to-strengthen-uk-capability/">one of 17</a> new UKRI Turing AI Pioneer Interdisciplinary Fellows! I'm the "AI mentor" (yes, that does sound silly) on his <a href="https://bsky.app/profile/eegcam.bsky.social/post/3n2jsfh6rgs2s">new project</a> on <em>"Predicting future biodiversity changes by integrating multiple lines of evidence at scale"</em>.</p>
<p><a href="https://toao.com">Sadiq Jaffer</a>, <a href="https://samreynolds.org">Sam Reynolds</a> and I have been enjoying working with Andrea's group on integrating our <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/papers/2025-evidence-tap">Evidence TAP</a> literature database. Andrea and <a href="https://calmaney.com">Calum Maney</a> from <a href="https://www.unep-wcmc.org/">UNEP-WCMC</a> are working on reverse parsing the graph layouts found in papers, which extends the <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/parsosaur">Parsosaur</a> extraction towards scientifically useful semi-structured outputs from illustrations in old papers. Graphs contain a lot of the historical quantitative evidence in ecology, so I'm looking forward to running this across the wider <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/2026w45">Taposaur</a> corpus as it matures.</p>
<h3><a href="https://anil.recoil.org/news.xml#switching-away-from-ceph-to-local-disks" class="anchor" aria-hidden="true"></a>Switching away from Ceph to local disks</h3>
<p>A few weeks ago I started moving some of my home directories onto our Ceph cluster to <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/2026w42">find out</a> if a network filesystem is practical to use these days. It's still very noticeably high latency...</p>
<p>For the tens of millions of small files in our paper corpus, it's difficult to use a network filesystem. <a href="https://www.tunbury.org/">Mark Elvers</a> has been <a href="https://www.tunbury.org/2026/11/09/week-45/">rebalancing the metadata servers</a> behind our large Ceph cluster, and <a href="https://www.tunbury.org/2026/11/16/week-46/">draining millions of strays</a>, but response times are still painfully slow for bulk processing. I'm switching over to local SSDs for the <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/papers/2025-evidence-tap">Evidence TAP</a> workload instead, with an offline rsync to Ceph to use as a live backup.</p>

<p>My Pembroke colleague <a href="https://albert.rierol.net/">Albert Cardona</a> also had an interesting thread on <a href="https://amok.recoil.org/@albertcardona@mathstodon.xyz/117664919456659868">what, if anything, has changed</a> in scientific publishing in the last 15 years. Back in 2011, the journal divisions of Elsevier, Springer and Wiley reported bumper profits, and researchers found that secrecy over 'big deal' contracts stopped universities realising <a href="https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1403006111">they were overpaying</a>. Albert reckons academics continue to foolishly judge papers by the journal prestige, and that open publishing really has to break through. I haven't heard much after the Royal Society's <a href="https://anil.recoil.org/notes/rs-future-of-publishing">future of publishing</a> meeting, so I wonder if this has petered out while publishing outputs grow exponentially.</p>
