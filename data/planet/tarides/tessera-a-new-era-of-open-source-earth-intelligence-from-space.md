title: 'Tessera: A New Era of Open-Source Earth Intelligence from Space'
description: 'We''ve all seen satellites drifting by at night, and wondered how many
  are actually up there. With how long they have been observing Earth, it is unsurprising
  that satellites have accumulated a vast library of images covering the globe. In
  a world increasingly focused on identifying and mitigating human impact on the natural
  world, these images constitute an untapped data resource.

  Sadly, most conservation groups, field biologists, and local policymakers don''t
  have a supercomputer on stand-by to help them trawl through that data and analyse
  trends. This is where the AI model Tessera, co-funded by Tarides and developed by
  a team headed by the University of Cambridge, comes in.

  What is Tessera?

  Tessera is an open-source AI foundation model which can parse through petabytes
  of satellite data to reveal patterns and changes over time. Tessera lets scientists
  designate habitat maps and highlight the changes they want to monitor over time.
  It can be used to reveal environmental changes, such as crop patterns, habitat loss,
  and deforestation.

  The software processes millions of satellite images from the European Space Agency
  into a compact, easily usable format for scientists. The result is that, instead
  of needing a supercomputer, researchers have access to global-scale analyses from
  their laptops.

  Tessera''s algorithm learns to understand the ''fingerprint'' of a landscape, knowing
  what it should look like, which allows it to fill in cloud-covered images with the
  missing values. For each 10-by-10-metre of land on Earth, the software creates an
  uninterpreted sequence of 128 numbers called ''embeddings''.  All of these embeddings
  are available on demand in the database GeoTessera.

  A recent example comes from the Scottish Highlands, where a Cambridge-led team used
  aerial surveys to build detailed 3D maps showing the beginnings of large-scale native
  woodland recovery across the Cairngorms National Park. Tarides supported that work,
  and the data feeds into Tessera. You can read the University of Cambridge write-up.

  To learn more about the project, you can read the article published by the University
  of Cambridge, the open access paper, and watch the webinar on Tessera.

  Tessera & Tarides: A Natural Fit with our Work in Space

  We are proud to have sponsored Tessera''s development, since it is open source and
  has no paywalls by design. As Tessera''s co-lead, Anil Madhavapeddy, professor of
  planetary computing at Cambridge, puts it: the goal is to "democratise satellite
  data for people who lack massive computer resources, whether that''s an underfunded
  conservation group here in the UK, field biodiversity researchers in the majority
  world, or planners figuring out climate adaptation strategies in a rapidly warming
  world."

  Tarides is dedicated to building secure and sustainable software with a positive
  impact, and supporting the Tessera project is a natural fit. It also complements
  our work on satellite software. Our sister company, Parsimoni, has been putting
  OCaml and MirageOS into orbit through SpaceOS, and we''ve built Notafs, a filesystem
  that lets Irmin run on MirageOS unikernels aboard satellites. Earlier this year,
  we also kicked off our part in the CEOS project, building next-generation Earth
  observation satellites.

  Tessera is a piece of that same puzzle; it''s one thing to get reliable, secure
  software into orbit and collect data, and another to make sense of what that data
  is telling us once it''s back on the ground. Whether it''s the satellite hardware
  and unikernels collecting the imagery, or a foundation model turning that imagery
  into insight for a conservation group with no cloud budget, the focus for us is
  the same: open tools should make space and Earth science more accessible, not less.
